How to remove a trustee in California comes down to one filing: a petition in the probate court under Probate Code § 17200. It asks the judge to remove the trustee, order them to repay what they took, and cut their fees. The court can do all three (Probate Code §§ 15642, 16420). It can also suspend the trustee's powers while the case is pending, so a sibling who is helping themselves does not keep the checkbook until trial.

Here's the thing families miss. Being a trustee is not ownership. A trustee holds your parent's property for the people named in the trust and owes them four duties: loyalty, prudence, impartiality, and information. Break one and the law calls it a breach of trust (Probate Code § 16400). The money claims run on a three-year clock (Probate Code § 16460), and it may already be ticking.

## A trustee owes you four duties, and here is what breaking each one looks like

A trustee must run the trust by its own terms (Probate Code § 16000) and meet four standards on top of that. Every trustee breach of fiduciary duty California courts act on falls under one of them.

### Loyalty: the trust is not the trustee's piggy bank

A trustee must administer the trust "solely in the interest of the beneficiaries" (Probate Code § 16002). No using trust property for the trustee's own profit, and no deals where the trustee's interest is adverse to yours (Probate Code § 16004). The trustee self-dealing California law forbids is not limited to theft. The examples I see most: the Sunnyvale house sold to a friend below market, "loans" from the trust to the trustee's business, the Cupertino condo rented to the trustee's daughter at half the going rate. And if the trustee makes a deal with you personally and comes out ahead, the law presumes a breach and makes the trustee prove otherwise (Probate Code § 16004).

### Prudence: careful, not clever

A trustee must act with "reasonable care, skill, and caution," the way a prudent person would run a similar enterprise (Probate Code § 16040). A Palo Alto house left empty and uninsured for two years is a prudence problem. So is a tax bill ignored until the penalties pile up. The trustee does not have to be a financial genius. The trustee does have to pay attention.

### Impartiality: no favorites among beneficiaries

With more than one beneficiary, the trustee must deal impartially with all of them (Probate Code § 16003). The trustee-sibling who pays themselves in January and tells you to wait until "things settle" is the everyday version. So is the one who lets a sibling live in the house rent-free while the others wait.

### Information: you are entitled to know what is going on

A trustee must keep beneficiaries "reasonably informed" (Probate Code § 16060) and account at least once a year to beneficiaries entitled to distributions (Probate Code § 16062). An account shows receipts, disbursements, assets, liabilities, the trustee's pay, and who the trustee hired (Probate Code § 16063). "Trust me" is not an account. Our guide on [getting an accounting or a copy of the trust](/library/trustee-wont-give-accounting-or-copy-of-trust-california/) covers the demand letter and the 60-day rule.

## You can sue for things the trustee did while your parent was alive

Any beneficiary can petition to remove a trustee, and so can a co-trustee or the settlor (Probate Code § 15642). The petition goes to the probate court under Probate Code § 17200, which covers passing on the trustee's acts, removing a trustee, and compelling redress of a breach by any available remedy.

Timing matters. While your parent is alive, competent, and able to revoke the trust, the trustee's duties run to your parent, not to you (Probate Code § 15800). Once your parent dies, that changes. In Estate of Giraldin the California Supreme Court held that beneficiaries "do have standing to sue for a breach of that duty after the settlor has died," including for what the trustee did while the parent was alive. The one limit: a trustee who followed the settlor's own directions violated no duty.

The claims are yours, not your parent's leftovers. In Asaro v. Maniscalco the court said beneficiaries have their own causes of action against trustees, "not derivative of the settlors' rights."

## The nine grounds for removing a trustee, and the two that surprise people

Probate Code § 15642 lists them:

- Breach of trust.
- Insolvency or unfitness to administer the trust.
- Hostility or lack of cooperation among co-trustees that impairs the administration.
- A trustee who "fails or declines to act."
- Excessive compensation.
- A sole trustee the law presumes should not take gifts under the document, such as the drafter or a care custodian of a dependent adult (Probate Code § 21380), unless the court finds the settlor truly chose them.
- Substantial inability to manage the trust's finances or do the job.
- Substantial inability to resist fraud or undue influence.
- "Other good cause."

Two of those catch readers off guard. Hostility among co-trustees is a ground on its own, no theft required. If you and your sister cannot agree on selling the house, the court can step in. And "fails or declines to act" means a trustee can be removed for doing nothing. Eighteen months with no sale, no distribution, no account, and no returned calls is not "being careful."

One caution. A petition filed in bad faith, where removal would be contrary to what the settlor wanted, can cost you the trustee's costs and attorney's fees (Probate Code § 15642). File because the trustee is breaking the rules, not because you are angry.

## What the court can order: repayment with interest, removal, and no pay

The remedies are in Probate Code § 16420. The court can compel the trustee to do the job, stop a threatened breach, and order repayment. It can appoint a receiver or temporary trustee, remove the trustee, and set aside the trustee's acts. It can reduce or deny the trustee's pay and trace property that was wrongfully disposed of.

The money order is called a surcharge. When you surcharge a trustee, the court charges the trustee personally for the trust's loss with interest, for any profit the trustee made with interest, and for profit the trust would have earned but for the breach (Probate Code § 16440). The court may excuse a trustee who acted reasonably and in good faith. Selling the house to a friend below market is not that.

### The three-year clock

A breach-of-trust claim must be filed within three years after you receive a written account or report that adequately discloses the problem. If you never got one, or it did not disclose the problem, the three years run from when you discovered or reasonably should have discovered it (Probate Code § 16460). Every formal account must warn you of this rule (Probate Code § 16063). The trap: "written report" is broader than a formal account. A letter with a spreadsheet attached can count, if it tells you enough to put you on notice.

Courts can be forgiving when a trustee hid the ball. In Asaro v. Maniscalco the trustees never sent the required notice that the trust had become irrevocable (Probate Code § 16061.7) and kept the beneficiary in the dark. The court held his claims timely because he had no reason to know of them earlier, and applied the delayed discovery rule. Do not count on that. Count three years from the first piece of paper you got.

### The death-of-trustee trap

If the trustee has died, the three years no longer control. Any claim you could have brought against the trustee while alive must be filed within one year of the trustee's death. That period "shall not be tolled or extended for any reason" beyond a few listed procedures (Code of Civil Procedure § 366.2). In Stoltenberg v. Newman the court applied the one-year rule to fraud claims based on a deceased trustor-trustee's statements, even though the suit named the successor trustee. Our [deadline guide](/library/how-long-do-i-have-to-contest-a-trust-or-will-in-california/) walks through the one-year rule.

## How to remove a trustee in California: the petition, the first hearing, and the replacement

In Santa Clara County, trust matters are heard by the Superior Court's Probate Division at 191 N. First Street in San Jose. People google "sample petition to remove trustee California" and try to file one themselves. Don't. The petition is a lawsuit: it lays out the facts, names the grounds under § 15642, and asks for specific orders. A typical one asks the court to:

1. Suspend the trustee's powers now and put the property in a temporary trustee's hands (Probate Code §§ 15642, 17206).
2. Order a full accounting, which the court can compel despite a waiver in the trust once a material breach looks reasonably likely (Probate Code § 16064).
3. Surcharge the trustee, cut the trustee's fees, set aside the bad transactions, and trace the property (Probate Code §§ 16420, 16440).
4. Remove the trustee and appoint a replacement (Probate Code § 17200).

### The first hearing

Removal itself is rarely decided at the first hearing. What can happen quickly is protection. If trust property or a beneficiary may suffer loss while the petition is pending, the court can order the trustee to hand the property to a co-trustee, receiver, or temporary trustee, and can suspend the trustee's powers (Probate Code § 15642). It can also appoint a temporary trustee to run the trust in whole or in part (Probate Code § 17206).

In Santa Clara County an emergency request can be presented ex parte, on shortened notice. The other side gets 24 hours' notice, and unless the matter needs same-day action the court allows 48 hours for an opposition (Probate Local Rule 2(B)(3)). The court's own rule says it plainly: "If there will be an objection, and the matter is not urgent, do not submit it ex parte." So the emergency has to be real: money moving, a house in escrow, a trustee who has gone silent.

### Who replaces the trustee

If the trust names a backup or gives a method for picking one, that controls. If not, the court appoints a trustee and must consider any nomination by beneficiaries who are at least 14 (Probate Code § 15660). In a contested Santa Clara County family the replacement is often a professional fiduciary rather than another sibling: a neutral who serves as trustee for a living under the Professional Fiduciaries Act (Business and Professions Code § 6501). That, in practice, is how to remove a trustee in California without handing the keys to the next sibling in line.

## What to do next

1. Write down what you know: dates, amounts, who said what. Keep every letter, statement, and text from the trustee.
2. Ask in writing, by mail and email, for a copy of the trust and a full accounting. If 60 days pass with nothing, the court can order both (Probate Code § 17200).
3. Pull the paper you can reach: deeds from the county recorder, bank statements, the trustee's emails.
4. Calendar the clocks: three years from any written report (Probate Code § 16460), one year from the trustee's death if the trustee died. If money was taken from your parent while alive, it may also be [financial elder abuse](/library/financial-elder-abuse-of-a-parent-california/).
5. Do not sign a receipt, release, or waiver until a lawyer has read it. A written waiver of accountings sticks until you withdraw it, and then only for future transactions (Probate Code § 16064).
6. Call a trust litigation lawyer this week, not next quarter.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a trustee be removed for being slow?

Yes, once slow has become "fails or declines to act" (Probate Code § 15642). The statute sets no number of months; the court asks whether the trust is actually being administered. A trustee must account at least yearly to beneficiaries entitled to distributions (Probate Code § 16062), so a year of nothing is already a missed duty. Grief explains a few months. It does not explain two years.

### Can I remove my brother as trustee if the trust names him?

Yes. The court can remove a named trustee on a beneficiary's petition (Probate Code § 15642); your parent's choice does not put him above the rules. The court does care what your parent wanted, though, and a bad-faith petition against that intent can cost you the trustee's fees. Bring evidence of a breach, not a grievance.

### Who pays the trustee's lawyer?

Trustees commonly pay their lawyer from the trust while a case is pending; whether that money comes back is decided at the end. The court can reduce or deny the trustee's own compensation (Probate Code § 16420). A removed disqualified trustee bears all costs including attorney's fees, and a beneficiary who files in bad faith can be ordered to pay the trustee's fees (Probate Code § 15642). Raise the fee question at your first meeting.

### Can the trustee be made to pay back money personally?

Yes. That is what a surcharge is: the trustee personally owes the trust's loss with interest, any profit the trustee made with interest, and profit the trust missed because of the breach (Probate Code § 16440). The court can also set aside the deal and trace the property to whoever holds it now (Probate Code § 16420). It is the trustee's own money on the line, which is usually when a stonewalling sibling starts returning calls.

### What if the trustee died?

Move fast. Any claim you could have brought against the trustee while alive must be filed within one year of the trustee's death (Code of Civil Procedure § 366.2), and Stoltenberg v. Newman applied that rule even though the suit named the successor trustee. The trust also needs a new trustee, by the document's method or by court appointment (Probate Code § 15660). Do not wait for the trustee's own estate to be "sorted out."

### What is the trustee fiduciary duty California law requires?

Four things. Loyalty: administer the trust solely in the beneficiaries' interest, never for the trustee's own profit (Probate Code §§ 16002, 16004). Prudence: reasonable care, skill, and caution (Probate Code § 16040). Impartiality: no favorites among beneficiaries (Probate Code § 16003). Information: keep beneficiaries reasonably informed and account at least yearly (Probate Code §§ 16060, 16062).
