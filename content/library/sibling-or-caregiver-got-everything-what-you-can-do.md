What to do if a sibling steals your inheritance depends on one fact most online answers skip: was the person who got everything family, or a caregiver? California treats the two differently. A gift to a caregiver is presumed to be the product of fraud or undue influence, and the caregiver must disprove it by clear and convincing evidence (Probate Code § 21380). A gift to a brother or sister is presumed nothing at all.

That does not mean the sibling keeps the house. You prove the case the older way, with the three-part test from Rice v. Clark, or with the red flags written into the undue influence statute. The evidence is the same either way: who found the new lawyer, who was in the room, and when the deed was recorded.

One number first. If the change is in a trust, you usually have 120 days from the day the trustee mails the formal notice (Probate Code § 16061.8).

## The caregiver rule: California presumes the gift was undue influence

A gift in a will, trust, or deed to a "care custodian" of a "dependent adult" is "presumed to be the product of fraud or undue influence." That applies if the document was signed while the caregiver was providing services, or within 90 days before or after (Probate Code § 21380(a)(3)). Lawyers call it Probate Code 21380. The caregiver's close relatives, cohabitants, and employees get the same treatment (Probate Code § 21380(a)(5), (6)).

The caregiver inheritance laws California applies turn on two defined terms. Expect the caregiver's lawyer to attack both.

- A care custodian is anyone who provides health or social services to a dependent adult: medicine, wound care, hygiene, companionship, housekeeping, shopping, cooking, or help with money (Probate Code § 21362). An agency aide qualifies. So can a neighbor who helped for free. In Bernard v. Foley, two friends took a woman into their home for her last weeks, changed her diapers, applied her medications, and handled her money. She amended her trust for them during that time. The California Supreme Court held they were care custodians. The statute now protects an unpaid helper whose friendship with your parent began at least 90 days before the care, at least six months before death, and before hospice (Probate Code § 21362(a)). An old friend is safe; a new one is not.
- A dependent adult is a person who, when signing, was 65 or older and either could not provide for their own health, food, clothing, or shelter, or had trouble managing money or resisting undue influence because of a mental deficit (Probate Code § 21366). A parent who was sharp and paying her own bills that day is probably not one. Then the presumption does not apply.

When it does apply, it bites. The caregiver can rebut it only "by clear and convincing evidence" (Probate Code § 21380(b)). A caregiver who fails "shall bear all costs of the proceeding, including reasonable attorney's fees" (Probate Code § 21380(d)). If the drafter of the document is the one who inherits, the presumption cannot be rebutted at all (Probate Code § 21380(c)).

A caregiver who also became trustee has a second problem. A sole trustee covered by § 21380 can be removed unless the court finds your parent truly wanted that person and was not unduly influenced (Probate Code § 15642(b)(6)). A sibling trustee is exempt from that ground. A caregiver removed on it pays the fees (Probate Code § 15642(c)), and any clause excusing that trustee from accounting is void (Probate Code § 16062(e)).

### The certificate of independent review, and why it is not the end

There is one escape hatch. An independent lawyer counsels your parent, with no heir or proposed beneficiary present, about the gift and its consequences, tries to determine whether it is the result of fraud or undue influence, and signs a certificate saying so. Then the presumption goes away (Probate Code § 21384). Expect the caregiver to produce one.

Then read it closely. In Estate of Winans, a man signed a new will a month before he died, with a new lawyer, leaving substantial property to his caregiver. There was a certificate. The counseling took "no more than one to five minutes," others were in the room, and the certifying lawyer was named executor. The Court of Appeal found triable issues and sent the case back.

## What to do if a sibling steals your inheritance: prove it the Rice v. Clark way

If you typed "sibling stole inheritance" into a search bar at two in the morning, this is your section. The statutory presumption does not apply to a person related to your parent by blood or marriage within the fourth degree, or to someone who lived with your parent (Probate Code § 21382(a)). A brother or sister is well inside that circle. Gifts of $5,000 or less from a larger estate are exempt too (Probate Code § 21382(e)).

But § 21382 turns off § 21380 and nothing else. The older, judge-made presumption still works. Under Rice v. Clark, a presumption of undue influence "shifting the burden of proof" arises when you show three things: the person "had a confidential relationship" with your parent; the person "actively participated in procuring the instrument's preparation or execution"; and the person "would benefit unduly" by it. In plain words:

1. **Trust.** Your parent relied on the sibling as caregiver, bookkeeper, or holder of a power of attorney.
2. **Procurement.** The sibling picked the lawyer, made the appointment, drove Dad there, or sat in the room.
3. **Undue benefit.** The result breaks from the plan your parent kept for years, and the sibling cannot explain why.

Show those three and the burden shifts. Your sibling must then prove the document was not procured by undue influence. That is how Hamlin v. Jendayi was won. A college professor went into hospice at the home of a former student two months before she died. There she signed a trust naming the student trustee and sole beneficiary. Her sisters proved all three elements, and in October 2024 the Court of Appeal affirmed the judgment throwing the trust out.

The other route is the statute that defines undue influence for everyone: "excessive persuasion that causes another person to act or refrain from acting by overcoming that person's free will and results in inequity" (Welfare and Institutions Code § 15610.70(a)). Our guide to [undue influence in California](/library/undue-influence-california-what-it-is-and-how-to-prove-it/) walks through its four factors.

## The red flags that decide these cases

The statute lists the tactics judges look for (Welfare and Institutions Code § 15610.70(a)(3)). The cases fill in the rest:

- Isolation: screened calls, turned-away visitors, a parent who only knew what one person told her.
- Control of medication, food, or sleep.
- A new lawyer, chosen by the person who benefited.
- Haste and secrecy: a signing nobody heard about until after the funeral.
- Changes "at inappropriate times and places": a hospital bed, a hospice room, a shipping store. In Keading v. Keading, a son took his ailing father to a UPS store to sign a new power of attorney. Eighteen days later he used it to deed the family house out of the trust to himself and his father as joint tenants. The father died five days after that. The son recorded the deed four days after the death. The judgment against him, more than $1.5 million, was affirmed.
- A power of attorney used to move money or amend the trust.
- The "helper" who ends up as trustee.
- A result that breaks sharply from the plan your parent kept for years.

One warning. "Evidence of an inequitable result, without more, is not sufficient to prove undue influence" (Welfare and Institutions Code § 15610.70(b)). A parent may favor one child. The case is tactics plus vulnerability plus result, never the result alone.

Silicon Valley makes these cases house-heavy. The estate is a Sunnyvale or Cupertino house bought in 1978, and the fight is a deed or amendment signed in the last year of life. Pull the recorded deed and compare the recording date with the date of death. In Santa Clara County that one document tells you what kind of case you have.

## What you can ask the court for

Most of this is asked for by petition in the probate court (Probate Code §§ 17200, 850). In one case you can ask for:

| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Set aside the amendment, will, or deed | The tainted document falls; the prior plan controls                                                                                                                                                                                                    | Probate Code §§ 17200(b)(3), 21380; Rice v. Clark                   |
| Remove the trustee                     | The court can also suspend the trustee's powers while the case is pending                                                                                                                                                                              | Probate Code §§ 15642, 17200(b)(10)                                 |
| Return of property                     | Recover the house or accounts held by the wrong person                                                                                                                                                                                                 | Probate Code § 850                                                  |
| Double damages                         | Twice the value of property taken in bad faith or through elder financial abuse                                                                                                                                                                        | Probate Code § 859                                                  |
| Attorney's fees                        | Mandatory against a caregiver who fails to rebut the presumption, and in any elder abuse case you win                                                                                                                                                  | Probate Code § 21380(d); Welfare and Institutions Code § 15657.5(a) |
| Treat the abuser as having died first  | On clear and convincing proof of financial abuse, bad faith, and related findings, the abuser gets no share of what the court awards back to the estate and cannot serve as trustee or executor under a document signed while your parent was impaired | Probate Code § 259                                                  |

Two notes. A trust or will change obtained by undue influence is itself financial elder abuse when your parent was 65 or older, because the statute counts property taken "by undue influence" and by "donative transfer, or testamentary bequest" (Welfare and Institutions Code §§ 15610.27, 15610.30). That opens the fee and double-damage doors against a sibling. And courts are split on double damages. Levin v. Winston-Levin requires proof of bad faith when the theory is undue influence. Keading v. Keading holds that no separate bad faith finding is needed when the theory is elder financial abuse. Our guide to [financial elder abuse of a parent](/library/financial-elder-abuse-of-a-parent-california/) covers the remedies in depth.

## What does not work

Anger does not work. Neither does a demand letter with no deadline in it; the other lawyer reads it as proof you have not filed. Waiting for probate to sort it out does not work either. The trustee's job is to carry out the document you say is invalid.

Filing late is worse than not filing. Raising § 21380 or undue influence is a "direct contest" (Probate Code § 21310(b)). A direct contest brought without probable cause triggers a no-contest clause (Probate Code § 21311). Our guide on [how to contest a trust in California](/library/how-to-contest-a-trust-in-california/) explains probable cause and the first hearing.

So what to do if a sibling steals your inheritance comes down to three things: a written request for the trust and an accounting, a hold on every document you can find, and a petition on file inside the deadline.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a caregiver inherit in California?

Yes, but a caregiver who inherits from a dependent adult is presumed to have used undue influence and must disprove it by clear and convincing evidence (Probate Code § 21380). The ways around it are a valid certificate of independent review (Probate Code § 21384) or proof that your parent was not a dependent adult when the document was signed.

### My sister was Mom's caregiver and is also her daughter. Does the presumption apply?

No. Relatives within the fourth degree are exempt no matter how much care they gave (Probate Code § 21382(a)). Your case against a sibling runs on the Rice v. Clark test and the undue influence factors instead. A sibling caregiver often meets the confidential relationship element on day one.

### What is a certificate of independent review?

A signed statement from an independent lawyer that he or she counseled your parent, with no heir or beneficiary in the room, about the gift and its consequences, tried to find out whether it was the product of fraud or undue influence, and concluded it was not (Probate Code § 21384). Estate of Winans shows that a rushed or compromised certificate can be challenged.

### What if the new will was drafted by the caregiver's friend?

Gifts to the drafter, and to the drafter's relatives, cohabitants, employees, and law partners, are presumed invalid, and for the drafter the presumption is conclusive (Probate Code § 21380(a)(1), (5), (6), (7), (c)). Against the caregiver, the friend's role is evidence: a lawyer supplied by the beneficiary points to active procurement under Rice v. Clark and to "haste or secrecy" under the statute.

### Can you sue a sibling for inheritance in California?

Yes. The petition asks the probate court to set aside the amendment or deed, remove your sibling as trustee, and order property returned (Probate Code §§ 17200, 15642, 850). If the facts add up to financial elder abuse, attorney's fees are mandatory if you win (Welfare and Institutions Code § 15657.5) and double damages are possible (Probate Code § 859).

### How long do I have?

In a trust case where you received the trustee's notice, 120 days from mailing (Probate Code § 16061.8). Financial elder abuse claims run four years from discovery (Welfare and Institutions Code § 15657.7). Any claim against a person who has died runs one year from their death (Code of Civil Procedure § 366.2). Get a date from the wizard, then call someone this week.
