Can you overturn a will or trust because your mother had dementia when she signed it? Sometimes. But the diagnosis alone will not do it. Testamentary capacity in California is a low bar, the law presumes she had it, and you carry the burden of proving she did not (Probate Code §§ 810, 8252).

The question a judge asks is narrow. Not whether she had dementia, but whether, at the moment she signed, she understood what the document was, what she owned, and who her family was (Probate Code § 6100.5). A person can fail a memory test on Monday and sign a valid will on Wednesday.

Here's the thing families get wrong. They collect proof that Dad was sick. The court wants proof of what Dad understood on one particular day. Those are different projects, and the second one has a deadline; the [deadline wizard](/how-long-do-i-have/) will tell you how many days are left.

## Testamentary capacity in California is a low bar, and you carry the burden

California law presumes that every person has the capacity to make decisions (Probate Code § 810). In a will contest, the person attacking the will must prove lack of capacity (Probate Code § 8252). Trusts are no different. In Doolittle v. Exchange Bank the Court of Appeal said a contestant carries a "heavy burden," and that the standard for testamentary capacity, in words borrowed from an earlier case, is "exceptionally low."

Why so low? Because the law wants people to leave their property as they choose, even when they are old, sick, forgetful, or difficult. A person with a mental or physical disorder may still be capable of executing wills or trusts, and a diagnosis by itself is not enough to prove incapacity (Probate Code §§ 810, 811).

So a Kaiser chart with a dementia diagnosis on it is a starting point, not a verdict. What wins is evidence of a specific deficit, tied to the specific decision, on the specific day (Probate Code § 811). Keep that sentence. It is the whole case.

## The will test in Probate Code 6100.5 asks three questions

Lack of testamentary capacity is the legal name for the claim. For a will, the test is Probate Code § 6100.5: a person is not competent to make a will if, at the time of making it, she cannot do any one of three things.

- Understand the nature of the testamentary act. Know that she is signing a document that says who gets her property when she dies.
- Understand and recollect the nature and situation of her property. Know, roughly, what she owns: the house in Sunnyvale, the brokerage account, the rental. Not the balances; the ability to handle business, even ordinary business, is not the test (Andersen v. Hunt, quoting older decisions).
- Remember and understand her relations to her living descendants, spouse, and parents, and to the people the will affects. Know who her children are, and grasp that the document cuts one of them out.

The statute has a second route. A will also fails if a mental health disorder with delusions or hallucinations caused her to leave property in a way she otherwise would not have (Probate Code § 6100.5). Think of a parent who becomes convinced a child is stealing from her when nothing of the kind happened, and disinherits that child for it. The delusion has to drive the gift.

What does not count: the courts have said for decades that "old age, feebleness, forgetfulness," eccentricity, and even "failure to recognize old friends or relatives" do not, by themselves, prove a lack of testamentary capacity (Andersen v. Hunt, quoting earlier cases). If your evidence is that Mom repeated herself and forgot names, you do not have a capacity case yet. You may have an undue influence case; more on that below.

## Capacity to make a trust in California runs on a sliding scale

Trusts and contracts are judged under a different part of the code, Probate Code 811 and its neighbors, sections 810 through 812. Section 811 lists the mental functions a court looks at: alertness and orientation, memory, understanding and communication, recognizing familiar people, reasoning and planning, thought processes such as delusions, and the ability to regulate mood. A finding of incapacity needs evidence of a deficit in at least one of them, and evidence that the deficit correlates with the decision in question (Probate Code § 811).

The deficit counts only if it significantly impairs the person's ability to understand and appreciate the consequences of that particular act (Probate Code § 811). Section 812 fills in what she must grasp: the rights and duties the decision creates, its probable consequences, and its significant risks, benefits, and reasonable alternatives (Probate Code § 812).

Here is why lawyers call this a sliding scale. The Court of Appeal in Andersen v. Hunt read those sections together and concluded that more complicated decisions require greater mental function and less complicated decisions require less. A one-page amendment that changes who gets what is a small decision. A long restated trust that creates sub-trusts, sorts community from separate property, and plans around estate tax is a big one. The same 84-year-old with the same chart might have capacity for the first and not the second.

## Which test applies depends on how complicated the document is

In Andersen v. Hunt, a father who had suffered a stroke amended his trust to leave 60 percent of his estate to his long-term partner, with the rest split among his children and a grandson. The amendments did nothing but set percentages, yet the probate court threw them out under the tougher sections 810 to 812 standard. The Court of Appeal reversed: an amendment that "in its content and complexity, closely resembles a will or codicil" gets the will test, and it sent the case back with directions to uphold the amendments.

In Lintz v. Lintz, a Monterey County case, a retired developer worth millions signed a string of amendments favoring his third wife, then a new trust her lawyer prepared. Those documents dealt with community property, gave the wife income for life, and created several trusts with estate tax planning at her death. Documents that complex, the court held, are judged under the sliding scale of sections 810 through 812, not the lower will test.

So the more the document tried to do, the more your parent had to understand to sign it. A one-paragraph amendment naming a caregiver as sole beneficiary gets the will test. A restated trust with a full tax plan inside it probably gets the sliding scale, and that can work in your favor. Read the document under attack before anything else.

## The moment of signing controls, and the law presumes a lucid interval

The will statute says at the time of making the will (Probate Code § 6100.5), and the cases say the same about trusts: the question is the signer's "mental state in relation to a specific event" (Andersen v. Hunt). That cuts both ways. A diagnosis in January does not prove incapacity in June. A good day in June does not rescue a document signed on a bad day in April.

Then there is the lucid interval rule, which surprises families. When a person has a mental disorder with lucid periods, the law presumes the will was made during one of them, and a finding of incapacity stands only if that presumption is overcome (Andersen v. Hunt, quoting earlier cases). Even a guardianship does not settle it; the older cases hold that a person under a guardianship can still make a valid will unless the incompetence continued at the time of signing (Andersen v. Hunt). The court may weigh "the frequency, severity, and duration of periods of impairment" (Probate Code § 811).

In practice, evidence from the week of the signing is worth ten times evidence from the year before. A nurse's note from the day before. The lawyer's notes from the signing meeting. Those are the exhibits.

## The evidence that wins a capacity case in Santa Clara County

Capacity cases here are decided by a judge in the Probate Division of the Santa Clara County Superior Court at 191 N. First Street in San Jose. There is no jury (Probate Code §§ 825, 8252). The judge reads the chart, hears the witnesses, and decides. That shapes what you gather.

- Medical records. In this county that means Stanford, Kaiser, El Camino, Good Samaritan, and Valley Medical, plus the primary care doctor and any memory clinic. You want the visit notes from the months around the signing, not just the diagnosis line. A Mini-Mental State Exam score, a nurse's note that the patient did not know the year, a discharge summary that says confused: those are deficits under Probate Code § 811, with a date attached.
- A medical witness who reads the chart. Usually nobody tested your parent's capacity on the day, and when someone did, it was at the request of the person who wanted the document signed. Both sides retain a geriatric psychiatrist or neuropsychologist to read the records and give an opinion about the signing date.
- The drafting lawyer's file. What the lawyer asked, what your parent answered, who booked the appointment, who drove her there, who sat in the room. The other side prepares for this too. In Doolittle v. Exchange Bank the mother, around the time she signed a trust moving most of her $8.5 million estate from her daughters to her gardener and a group of friends and caregivers, had a neuropsychologist sign an affidavit that she had capacity that day, obtained six certificates of independent review from a separate lawyer, and left written instructions to pay her doctor and lawyer for their time testifying. The court also let the trustee spend trust money defending the amendment while the daughter's contest was pending.
- Witnesses. The notary, the witnesses to the will, the neighbor who visited that week, the home health aide. A caregiver's daily log is gold.
- Conservatorship files. Conservatorship cases are heard in the same Probate Division, and if anyone asked the court to appoint a conservator for your parent, that file is dated evidence. Remember the rule above, though: a finding about your parent's abilities in March is not proof about a signature in June.

### Capacity and undue influence travel together

Almost every capacity case is also an undue influence case, and you should plead both. The statute that defines undue influence lists impaired cognitive function, illness, isolation, and dependency as evidence of a victim's vulnerability (Welfare and Institutions Code § 15610.70; Probate Code § 86 adopts the same definition). The same chart that falls short on capacity can carry the vulnerability factor. Lintz is the proof: the trial court found the husband had capacity and voided his trust documents anyway, because his wife had unduly influenced him. Our guide to [undue influence in California](/library/undue-influence-california-what-it-is-and-how-to-prove-it/) covers the four factors and the presumptions.

## What to do next

1. Find the document and its date. The signing date is the center of the case. Get the amendment or will, the notary block, and any cover letter that came with it.
2. Calendar the deadline today. A lack of capacity claim is a direct contest (Probate Code § 21310). After a trustee's notice you have 120 days to file it (Probate Code § 16061.8); for a will, object before it is admitted or petition to revoke within 120 days after (Probate Code §§ 8250, 8270). A late contest counts as one filed without probable cause, which can trigger a no-contest clause and cost you everything (Probate Code § 21311; Meiri v. Shamtoubi). Our [deadline guide](/library/how-long-do-i-have-to-contest-a-trust-or-will-in-california/) has every clock.
3. List every doctor, hospital, and clinic that saw your parent in the year around the signing. Your lawyer will get the records through the case.
4. Write down what you saw, with dates. The day she did not know your name. The repeated questions. Who drove her to the lawyer.
5. Identify the people in the room: the lawyer, the notary, the witnesses, the caregiver. Give the names to counsel rather than calling them yourself.
6. Call a lawyer this week. The clock is running, and the other side, as Doolittle shows, may already have a file built.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can someone with dementia make a valid will in California?

Yes, often. A person with a mental or physical disorder may still be capable of executing wills or trusts (Probate Code § 810), and a diagnosis alone does not prove otherwise (Probate Code § 811). Families ask about dementia and signing a will more than any other capacity question, and the answer is always the same: what did she understand on the day she signed?

### Does a doctor's letter decide testamentary capacity?

No. A letter saying your parent had capacity, or lacked it, is one piece of evidence; what carries weight is the underlying chart, the test scores, and their timing (Probate Code § 811). In Doolittle the mother herself obtained a neuropsychologist's affidavit around the time she signed. That did not end her daughter's contest; it became evidence in it.

### Who has the burden of proof on testamentary capacity in California?

You do, if you are contesting. The law presumes capacity (Probate Code § 810), the will contest statute puts the burden on the contestant (Probate Code § 8252), and the courts call it a heavy burden for trusts as well (Doolittle v. Exchange Bank). Undue influence, if you plead it too, must be proven by clear and convincing evidence (Doolittle v. Exchange Bank).

### What if the lawyer who drafted the trust says Mom was fine?

Then the lawyer is a witness, an important one, but not the judge. Ask what the lawyer did to test it: how long the meeting ran, who else was in the room, whether Mom explained in her own words what she owned and who she was leaving out. A lawyer who met her for fifteen minutes with the new beneficiary beside her has handed you evidence too.

### Is a trust harder to overturn than a will for lack of capacity?

It depends on the document, not the label. A short amendment that only changes who gets what is judged by the same low will test (Andersen v. Hunt). A complex trust that restructures property and tax planning is judged on a sliding scale that demands more mental function for bigger decisions (Lintz v. Lintz). In that narrow sense a complicated trust can be easier to attack. Our guide to [contesting a trust in California](/library/how-to-contest-a-trust-in-california/) covers the procedure.

### How long do I have to file a lack of testamentary capacity claim?

The ground does not change the clock. For a trust, 120 days from the day the trustee mails the formal notice after the death (Probate Code § 16061.8); if no notice ever came, the outer limit is unsettled, which is a reason to hurry, not relax. For a will, object before it is admitted or petition to revoke within 120 days after (Probate Code §§ 8250, 8270). Missing the date can also trigger a no-contest clause (Meiri v. Shamtoubi). The [deadline wizard](/how-long-do-i-have/) gives you a date.
