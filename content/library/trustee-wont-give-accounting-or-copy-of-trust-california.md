The short answer is yes, you are entitled to all of it. After a parent dies with a living trust, every beneficiary gets a formal notice from the trustee within 60 days, a true and complete copy of the trust on request, and an accounting at least once a year. A trust accounting in California is a legal duty, not a favor (Probate Code § 16062). If you ask in writing and the trustee stalls for 60 days, the probate court can order it (Probate Code § 17200).

Here's the thing most families in Silicon Valley get wrong. They ask by phone, nicely, for months. A phone call starts nothing. A dated letter starts a 60-day clock that ends in front of a judge, and a trustee with something to hide knows it.

## What the trustee owes you, and when

California puts the duty in one sentence: the trustee "has a duty to keep the beneficiaries of the trust reasonably informed of the trust and its administration" (Probate Code § 16060). Beneficiary rights to trust accounting, information, and a copy of the document all hang off that sentence.

| What you are owed                                             | When                                                               | Who gets it                                       | Rule                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| The trustee's formal notice (the Probate Code 16061.7 notice) | Within 60 days of the death                                        | Every beneficiary and every heir                  | Probate Code § 16061.7 |
| A true and complete copy of the trust                         | On request, after the death                                        | Any beneficiary or heir                           | Probate Code § 16061.5 |
| Information about the administration                          | On reasonable request                                              | Beneficiaries                                     | Probate Code § 16061   |
| A full accounting                                             | At least yearly, when the trust ends, and when the trustee changes | Beneficiaries entitled to income or principal now | Probate Code § 16062   |

Two details matter. The notice must tell you that you can ask for a copy of the trust (Probate Code § 16061.7(g)(5)), so the trustee's own letter is your invitation. And "heir" is a legal word: anyone who would inherit under state law if there were no will or trust (Probate Code § 44). For a parent, that usually means the children.

So if you were cut out of the trust, you still get the notice and the copy. The law wants you to see the document you might contest. One exception: a living trust signed before July 1, 1987 is exempt from the yearly accounting duty (Probate Code § 16062(b)). The duty to keep you informed and answer reasonable requests still applies.

## What a real trust accounting in California contains

A spreadsheet titled "what's left" is not an accounting. Neither is a bank balance read to you over the phone. An account must contain six things (Probate Code § 16063):

1. Receipts and disbursements of principal and income since the last account, or for the last complete fiscal year.
2. The trust's assets and liabilities at the end of that period.
3. The trustee's compensation for the period.
4. The agents the trustee hired, their relationship to the trustee, and what they were paid.
5. A statement that you may petition the court under Section 17200 to review the account and the trustee's acts.
6. A statement that claims against the trustee for breach of trust may not be made more than three years after you receive an account or report disclosing the facts.

Read the last two again. A real accounting has to tell you how to challenge it and warn you that a clock is running. If the paper in your hand does neither, it is not the accounting the law requires, though it may still have started the three-year clock covered below.

The numbers should let you trace every dollar from the date of death to today. Where did the proceeds from the Cupertino house go? Who was paid, and for what? A trustee who kept records can answer that from the file.

## How to ask: the letter that starts the 60-day clock

The court's power to compel turns on a written request. The court can order an accounting when the trustee "has failed to submit a requested account within 60 days after written request of the beneficiary" and no account was made in the six months before the request (Probate Code § 17200(b)(7)). Information requests work the same way. So put it in writing, date it, and keep proof that it was sent.

Here is the paragraph that does the work. Change the names and dates and nothing else.

> I am a beneficiary and heir of the [Name] Family Trust. Under Probate Code sections 16061.5 and 16061.7, please send me a true and complete copy of the trust and every amendment. Under sections 16061 and 16062, please also send me an accounting from [date of death] to today, with the receipts, disbursements, assets, and liabilities that section 16063 requires, and the bank and brokerage statements behind them. I am sending this letter by certified mail and email on [date]. If I have not received these items within 60 days, I will ask the Santa Clara County Superior Court to order them under Probate Code section 17200.

Send it to the trustee's address on the notice, and to the trustee's lawyer if one has surfaced. The date on the letter is day one. Then do the hard part: wait the 60 days without calling to argue. Every angry text is a chance for the trustee to claim the request was unclear or withdrawn.

## What the probate court in San Jose can do

Trust disputes for Santa Clara County are heard by the Superior Court's Probate Division at 191 N. First Street in San Jose. The tool is a Probate Code 17200 petition, which covers "the internal affairs of the trust" (Probate Code § 17200(a)). When a trustee refuses to provide accounting records, the petition asks the court for some or all of these:

1. An order compelling a copy of the trust terms (Probate Code § 17200(b)(7)(A)).
2. An order compelling the information or the accounting you requested (Probate Code § 17200(b)(7)(B), (C)).
3. Instructions to the trustee, which can include a date the accounting is due (Probate Code § 17200(b)(6)).
4. Removal of the trustee and appointment of a replacement (Probate Code § 17200(b)(10)). Grounds include breach of trust and a trustee who "fails or declines to act" (Probate Code § 15642(b)).
5. Redress for a breach of trust "by any available remedy" (Probate Code § 17200(b)(12)).

Refusing to account is a breach, not a paperwork lapse. A violation of any duty the trustee owes you is a breach of trust (Probate Code § 16400), and keeping you informed is one of those duties. The remedies include an order to perform, removal, reduced or denied compensation, and a temporary trustee to take possession of the property (Probate Code § 16420).

Silence is also evidence. A trustee who spends a year dodging a request for bank statements has told the court something about the bank statements. Bring the letters, the certified-mail receipts, and the emails that went unanswered.

Skipping the notice has its own price. A trustee who never served it "shall be responsible for all damages, attorney's fees, and costs caused by the failure" unless the trustee made a reasonably diligent effort to comply (Probate Code § 16061.9).

## The trap: paperwork you already have may have started a three-year clock

Here's the part that surprises people. A claim against a trustee for breach of trust is barred three years after you receive "an interim or final account in writing, or other written report, that adequately discloses the existence of a claim" (Probate Code § 16460). If nothing you received disclosed it, or you got nothing at all, the three years run from when you discovered, or reasonably should have discovered, the problem.

A "written report" does not have to be a proper accounting; the statute says it need not meet the requirements of Section 16061 or 16063 (Probate Code § 16460(c)). A letter with a few numbers in it, or a stack of statements with a sticky note, can count if it gave you enough to know of the claim or to have asked about it. So the half-answer you got last year matters, and so does its date.

Courts do take concealment seriously. In Asaro v. Maniscalco the trustees never sent the notice that the trust had become irrevocable, and their conduct kept a beneficiary from learning of his claims against them. The court applied the delayed discovery rule and held his claim was timely. Good law for beneficiaries, but it took years of litigation to get there.

The second clock is the contest clock, and it does not wait for the document. The notice with the bold warning started 120 days to challenge the trust itself for undue influence, lack of capacity, fraud, or forgery (Probate Code § 16061.8). A copy delivered inside that window gives you at least 60 days from delivery, but a copy that never arrives pauses nothing. A notice whose warning was printed in the same plain type as the rest did not start the clock at all (Harustak v. Wilkins).

Filing late is worse than filing without the document. In Meiri v. Shamtoubi a contest filed months after the deadline was treated as one filed without probable cause, and the daughter who filed it lost her entire inheritance under the trust's no-contest clause. The [deadline wizard](/how-long-do-i-have/) gives you the date; our guide to [how long you have to contest a trust or will](/library/how-long-do-i-have-to-contest-a-trust-or-will-in-california/) covers the exceptions.

## What to do next

1. Find the trustee's notice. Note the date it was mailed and whether the 120-day warning is in bold.
2. Send the demand letter above today, by certified mail and email. Save the receipts.
3. Calendar day 60. If nothing has arrived, a petition to compel is available.
4. Gather everything the trustee already sent you, with dates. One of those papers may have started a three-year clock.
5. Call a lawyer before day 60 if the trustee is also the person who gained the most from a late amendment, or if a house has already been sold. If the accounting shows self-dealing, the next step is a [petition to surcharge and remove the trustee](/library/trustee-breach-of-fiduciary-duty-and-removal-california/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Am I entitled to a copy of the trust if I was left out of it?

Yes, if you are an heir. The trustee must serve the notice on each heir of the deceased settlor as well as every beneficiary (Probate Code § 16061.7), and must give a copy of the trust terms to any heir who asks (Probate Code § 16061.5). Here is how to get a copy of a trust in California when you were cut out: send the written request, cite those two sections, and give the 60-day deadline. The copy is what you need to decide whether to [contest the trust](/library/how-to-contest-a-trust-in-california/).

### Can the trust waive accountings?

Sometimes on paper, rarely in the end. A trust can say the trustee need not account, and a beneficiary can waive the right in writing, but "upon a showing that it is reasonably likely that a material breach of the trust has occurred, the court may compel the trustee to account" regardless (Probate Code § 16064). A beneficiary's written waiver can be withdrawn for future transactions. And a waiver in the trust is void as to a sole trustee the law presumes to have used undue influence, such as the person who drafted the trust or a care custodian (Probate Code §§ 16062(e), 21380).

### How long does the trustee have to respond to my request?

Sixty days. That is how long a trustee has after your written request for a trust accounting in California before the court's power to compel is available, and only if you got no account in the six months before you asked (Probate Code § 17200(b)(7)). Information requests get the same 60 days. No statute makes a trustee answer a phone call. A trustee with nothing to hide usually responds much sooner.

### Can I get the actual bank statements, not a summary?

Ask for them in the same letter. On reasonable request the trustee must provide information "relating to the administration of the trust relevant to the beneficiary's interest" (Probate Code § 16061), and the account itself must show receipts, disbursements, assets, and liabilities (Probate Code § 16063). The judge decides what is reasonable, not the trustee, and statements behind an accounting after a death are about as reasonable as requests get.

### Does asking for an accounting count as contesting the trust?

No. A no-contest clause can be enforced against a direct contest filed without probable cause and, in narrow cases the clause must spell out, against a creditor's claim or a claim that property never belonged to the parent (Probate Code § 21311). A direct contest is a pleading that attacks the trust's validity for forgery, lack of capacity, undue influence, and the like (Probate Code § 21310). A letter asking for a copy or an accounting is not a pleading, and a petition to compel an accounting does not attack the trust's validity. Every clause is worded differently, so have a lawyer read yours before you file.

### Can I demand an accounting while my parent is still alive?

Usually not. While the trust can be revoked and the person who can revoke it is competent, that person holds the beneficiary's rights and the trustee's duties run to them, not to you (Probate Code § 15800). If no one who can revoke the trust is competent, the trustee has 60 days from learning of it to send you a copy of the trust, and must account to you at least yearly (Probate Code § 15800(b)). And after the death you can sue for breaches the trustee committed while your parent was alive (Estate of Giraldin).
