What is undue influence? The short answer: excessive persuasion that overcomes a person's free will and produces an unfair result. That is the undue influence California statute in one sentence (Probate Code § 86; Welfare and Institutions Code § 15610.70). If a caregiver, a sibling, or a new spouse pushed your parent into signing a will, a trust amendment, or a deed they would never have signed alone, this is the claim.

Here's the thing families get wrong. Undue influence is not a feeling. It is a set of facts you prove with records, and the law puts the burden on you (Probate Code § 8252). A frail parent, a helper who ran the house, a lawyer nobody had heard of, and a document that flipped a lifelong plan: that is a case. A parent who simply liked one child more: that is not.

## The undue influence California statute: one definition, four factors

Since 2014 California has used one definition in probate court and in elder abuse cases. Probate Code § 86 adopts it from the Welfare and Institutions Code word for word. Undue influence "means excessive persuasion that causes another person to act or refrain from acting by overcoming that person's free will and results in inequity" (Welfare and Institutions Code § 15610.70).

The statute lists four things a court "shall" consider. Score your own facts against each one:

1. **Vulnerability.** Illness, disability, age, impaired thinking, emotional distress, isolation, or dependency, and whether the influencer knew. The widow on hospice, alone with one helper, is the textbook case.
2. **Apparent authority.** A fiduciary, family member, care provider, doctor, lawyer, or spiritual adviser. An agent under a power of attorney fits squarely.
3. **Tactics.** The red flags in the next section.
4. **Equity of the result.** What it cost your parent, how far it strays from the earlier plan, and whether the change fits the relationship.

No single factor decides it; a judge weighs all four. The court in Lintz v. Lintz said the 2014 law "eliminates any doubt that the two standards are now the same" in probate and elder abuse cases. So the same test applies to a trust, a will, a deed, or an emptied bank account. A will, or any part of one, "procured by duress, menace, fraud, or undue influence" is ineffective (Probate Code § 6104), and courts void trusts on the same ground.

## Red flags: the tactics the statute names

Section 15610.70 spells out the tactics. Read the list against what happened in your family:

- **Control.** The statute's first example is controlling "necessaries of life, medication, the victim's interactions with others, access to information, or sleep" (Welfare and Institutions Code § 15610.70). Picture the helper who screens the phone and drives to every appointment.
- **Affection, intimidation, or coercion.** Sudden devotion from a new friend, or threats to put a parent in a home.
- **Haste and secrecy.** Property changes made in a hurry, "at inappropriate times and places," by someone with "claims of expertise" (same section). A new lawyer, a hospital-room signing, and nobody told the family until after the funeral.

The classic undue influence trust amendment in Silicon Valley moves a Sunnyvale or Cupertino house to one person, weeks before death, through a lawyer the parent never used. Hamlin v. Jendayi (2024) shows the pattern. A college professor went into hospice at a former student's home two months before she died. The student held her power of attorney, controlled her food and care, and called a lawyer to draw up a trust naming the student sole beneficiary. The probate court found the professor vulnerable and completely dependent, voided the trust, and the Court of Appeal affirmed.

## The presumption of undue influence California law gives you

In an undue influence California contest, the court expects you to carry the burden. Two presumptions flip it.

**The statutory presumption.** Probate Code § 21380 presumes a gift in a will or trust is "the product of fraud or undue influence" when it goes to the person who drafted it, a fiduciary who transcribed it, or a care custodian of a dependent adult. For the care custodian, the document must have been signed during the care or within 90 days of it. Their close relatives, cohabitants, and employees are covered too. The recipient must disprove undue influence by clear and convincing evidence, and if they fail they pay everyone's attorney's fees (Probate Code § 21380). For the drafter, the presumption cannot be rebutted at all.

A dependent adult here is, roughly, a person 65 or older who could not meet basic needs or had trouble resisting undue influence because of a mental deficit (Probate Code § 21366). A care custodian is anyone providing health or social services to that person, down to "companionship, housekeeping, shopping, cooking, and assistance with finances" (Probate Code § 21362). In Bernard v. Foley the California Supreme Court held that unrelated friends who nursed a dying woman in their home were care custodians, friendship or not. The Legislature later exempted unpaid friends whose relationship predates the care by set periods (Probate Code § 21362), so the timeline matters.

The statutory presumption does not reach relatives within the fourth degree or someone who lived with your parent (Probate Code § 21382). A sibling who inherits everything is not presumed anything. That is the second presumption's job; see [what you can do when a sibling or caregiver got everything](/library/sibling-or-caregiver-got-everything-what-you-can-do/).

**The common-law presumption.** The California Supreme Court restated it in Rice v. Clark. The burden shifts when you show three things: the influencer "had a confidential relationship with the testator"; the influencer "actively participated in procuring the instrument's preparation or execution"; and the influencer "would benefit unduly by the testamentary instrument" (Rice v. Clark). It works against anyone: a sibling, a second spouse, a new best friend. Confidential relationship means trust and dependence, like the person paying Mom's bills or holding her power of attorney. Active participation means picking the lawyer, making the appointment, sitting in the room; undue benefit is read against the earlier plan and the family tree.

Once either presumption applies, the person who got the gift must prove the document was not the product of undue influence, and under the statute that proof must be clear and convincing (Probate Code § 21380). Without a presumption, the burden of proof for undue influence in California stays on you, and it is heavy. In Doolittle v. Exchange Bank the court repeated the rule: the contestant "bears the burden of proving undue influence" and must prove it "by clear and convincing evidence" (Doolittle v. Exchange Bank).

## What undue influence is not

An unequal estate plan, by itself. The statute says so: "Evidence of an inequitable result, without more, is not sufficient to prove undue influence" (Welfare and Institutions Code § 15610.70). A parent may leave more to the child who moved home and less to the one who calls at Christmas. A parent may change their mind. The statutory presumption exempts relatives on purpose (Probate Code § 21382).

Persuasion, by itself. Adult children lobby their parents about money all the time. The word in the statute is "excessive," and what makes lobbying excessive is the combination: a vulnerable parent, a person with authority over them, tactics from the list above, and a result that does not fit.

Lack of capacity. That is a different claim with a different test. A parent can understand exactly what they are signing and still sign because someone overcame their will. In Hamlin the probate court found the professor did not lack capacity and still voided the trust. The two claims usually travel together; see our guide to [lack of testamentary capacity in California](/library/lack-of-testamentary-capacity-california/).

## How to prove undue influence in California: build the record

Proof is built, not asserted. There is no jury in probate court unless a statute provides one (Probate Code § 825). A judge in San Jose decides, and the judge reads records. Here is where the evidence lives in a Santa Clara County contest:

- **Medical records.** Charts from Stanford, Kaiser, El Camino, Good Samaritan, or Valley Medical near the signing date. They prove vulnerability and often prove the influencer knew.
- **The drafting lawyer's file.** Who called for the appointment, who paid, who sat in the room, whether the lawyer ever met your parent alone.
- **Bank and property records.** Statements, new joint accounts, deeds recorded with the Santa Clara County Recorder in the last months of life, transactions under a power of attorney.
- **The influencer's own words.** Texts, emails, voicemails, posts. People who isolate a parent usually document it.
- **Witnesses and earlier plans.** The regular doctor, the old lawyer, the friend who stopped being allowed to visit, and every prior will and trust.

The other side builds a record too. In Doolittle the mother had obtained six certificates of independent review before she died; the trustee used them to defend the amendment, and the court let the trust pay for the defense. Then plan for a real trial: the Hamlin contest ran 17 days in front of a judge.

## What to do next

1. **Preserve everything.** Do not clean out the house. Photograph medication bottles, calendars, and notes on the fridge. Save texts and voicemails.
2. **Request the trust in writing.** A beneficiary or heir who asks after a death is entitled to a full copy of the trust terms (Probate Code § 16061.5).
3. **Calendar the deadline.** If the trustee mailed you a formal notice, you have 120 days from the mailing date to file a trust contest (Probate Code § 16061.8). Read [how long you have to contest a trust or will](/library/how-long-do-i-have-to-contest-a-trust-or-will-in-california/).
4. **List the medical providers.** Every doctor, hospital, and hospice from the last two years, with dates. Your lawyer will subpoena the charts.
5. **Call a lawyer this week.** Bring the new document, the old one, the trustee's letter, and anything in the influencer's own words.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is undue influence in plain English?

Someone used pressure, manipulation, or control to get a vulnerable person to sign something they would not have signed on their own, and the result is unfair. The statute calls it "excessive persuasion" that overcomes free will and "results in inequity" (Welfare and Institutions Code § 15610.70). It is a civil claim decided by a judge, and winning usually means the will, trust, or deed is thrown out.

### Is undue influence a crime?

Not by itself. It is a civil wrong, and because taking an elder's property by undue influence is financial elder abuse (Welfare and Institutions Code § 15610.30), the elder abuse statutes add remedies. Theft, embezzlement, forgery, or fraud against a person 65 or older is a separate crime (Penal Code § 368) that the District Attorney prosecutes. A police report does not get the house back; the civil case does. See our guide to [financial elder abuse of a parent](/library/financial-elder-abuse-of-a-parent-california/).

### Can a spouse be guilty of undue influence?

Yes, and the law is harder on spouses than most people expect. Spouses owe each other "the highest good faith and fair dealing" (Family Code § 721). In Lintz v. Lintz a third wife had her husband convert his separate property to community property and sign trusts that disinherited two of his three children. The Court of Appeal held that when one spouse gains an advantage in a transaction, a presumption of undue influence arises and that spouse must rebut it. The trust documents stayed voided.

### Does a certificate of independent review end the case?

No. A certificate is a lawyer's signed statement that they counseled your parent away from the beneficiary and concluded the gift was not the product of fraud or undue influence. It only takes the gift out of the statutory presumption (Probate Code § 21384). You can still prove undue influence the ordinary way, and the certificate itself can be attacked. In Estate of Winans the counseling took "no more than one to five minutes," others were in the room, and the lawyer was named executor; the Court of Appeal found triable issues and reversed the summary judgment.

### What if my parent seemed fine?

They can be fine and still be a victim. Capacity asks whether your parent understood the document; undue influence asks whether the choice was really theirs. In Hamlin v. Jendayi the probate court found the professor did not lack capacity and still voided the trust. Vulnerability under the statute includes illness, isolation, emotional distress, and dependency, none of which requires dementia (Welfare and Institutions Code § 15610.70). The people who say a parent seemed fine are often the ones who were kept away.

### How long do I have to bring an undue influence claim?

For a trust, 120 days from the day the trustee mails the formal notice after death (Probate Code § 16061.8). For a will, object before it is admitted to probate or petition within 120 days after (Probate Code § 8270). Financial elder abuse runs four years from discovery (Welfare and Institutions Code § 15657.7), but only one year from death if the influencer has died (Code of Civil Procedure § 366.2). Filing a trust contest late is worse than not filing: in Meiri v. Shamtoubi a daughter who sued about 230 days after the notice lost her whole inheritance under the no-contest clause (Probate Code § 21311).
